Adapted Tree-Sliced Wasserstein:
a path-only estimator for adapted optimal transport,
with a machine-checked consistency proof
[authors]
[affiliations]
Working draft — September 6, 2026
Abstract
Adapted (bicausal) Wasserstein distances respect the information flow of a stochastic process,
but every published algorithm for computing them either fixes a scenario tree in advance or
requires access to the transition kernel. We introduce Adapted Tree-Sliced Wasserstein (ATSW),
an estimator that works from sampled paths alone: it quantises paths on a fixed grid, conditions
on a window of the last k steps, replaces the optimal-transport problem at each node pair by a
closed-form comonotone coupling, and averages over random grid shifts. On the scenario trees
used by prior work ATSW is three to four orders of magnitude cheaper than exact dynamic
programming, at the cost of a coupling-induced bias that we measure and do not yet bound.
Our main negative finding is that a path-only estimator conditioning on the full history is
statistically infeasible at depth: its bias decays like n−0.19, so five per cent accuracy would need
on the order of 109 paths. Conditioning on a k-step window removes this: on a benchmark with
analytic ground truth the bias falls from +42.4% to +1.24%, below a model-based competitor
that uses strictly more information. We prove that the estimator is consistent, and the proof
is machine-checked in Lean 4; formalisation forced three assumptions into the open that an
informal argument would have left implicit. We also show that the estimator’s limit law is not
Gaussian at parameter values where two c.d.f.s coincide, so naive normal confidence intervals
are mis-centred rather than merely mis-scaled.
Keywords. adapted Wasserstein distance; bicausal optimal transport; nested distance; comono-
tone coupling; formal verification.

Introduction
1.1
Information flow is not captured by Wasserstein distance
Let µ, ν be laws of discrete-time processes on RT . The Wasserstein distance W(µ, ν) treats a path
as a point in RT and therefore cannot see when information becomes available.
The standard
two-period example makes this concrete. Let µ reveal a signal of size ε at time 1 and then move to
±1 determined by that signal; let ν reveal nothing at time 1 and then move to ±1 by a fair coin.
As ε →0 the two laws become indistinguishable in W, yet they are entirely different as filtered
objects: under µ a decision maker knows the terminal value one step early, under ν she never does.
The adapted distance separates them by 1 + ε while W reports ε.

This matters wherever decisions are sequential: multistage stochastic optimisation, scenario
tree generation and reduction, evaluation of generative models for time series, and reward shaping
from expert trajectories.
1.2
Computation is the obstacle
Exact adapted transport is a dynamic program over pairs of nodes of two scenario trees. The
published methods fall into two families, and neither matches the situation a practitioner most
often faces — a finite collection of sampled paths and nothing else.
 Tree-given methods take two scenario trees as input and solve the nested recursion, exactly
or with entropic regularisation. They say nothing about where the tree came from.
 Model-given methods sample successor states on demand, which requires the transition
kernel. Fitted value iteration is of this type: its inner loop draws successors from the true
kernel.
Table 1 collects the published operating points. No entry reports both a large horizon and a large
sample size.
1.3
Contributions
1. Readout, not structure, determines the recovered topology (Section 4.1). A time-
first tree is necessary but not sufficient: on the same tree, a flat tree-Wasserstein readout
converges to the wrong limit, a single lexicographic sweep collapses to static Wasserstein, and
only a nested readout recovers the adapted distance.
2. The ATSW estimator (Section 4): fixed-grid quantisation, k-step conditioning, closed-form
comonotone couplings, and averaging over grid shifts. Its cost is output-sensitive.
3. An informational, not algorithmic, limit (Section 6.2). With paths only and full-history
conditioning the bias decays like n−0.19. Two natural repairs (coarsening with depth; data-
adaptive cells) both fail, and we explain why.
4. Consistency, machine-checked (Section 5). A Lean 4 development with no sorry, de-
pending only on Lean’s three standard axioms. Formalisation forced three hypotheses into
the open.
5. A non-Gaussian limit law at ties (Section 5.3), with a minimal example, a hand deriva-
tion, and a simulation that rejects normality.
1.4
Organisation
Section 2 positions the work. Section 3 fixes notation. Section 4 defines the estimator, Section 5
states and proves what we can prove, Section 6 reports experiments, Section 7 the demonstration
application, and Section 8 discusses limitations.

Related work
2.1
Theory of adapted transport
The adapted (nested) distance was introduced to make multistage stochastic programs stable under
perturbation of the underlying process.
On the real line and for monotone costs the Knothe–
Rosenblatt coupling is optimal among causal couplings, which is what makes a quantile-based
surrogate plausible in the first place. A separate line studies adapted empirical measures — how
fast an empirical process converges in adapted distance — and it is this line that our consistency
result speaks to.
2.2
Computation of adapted transport
2.3
Sliced and tree-sliced transport
A large literature obtains O(Ln log n) transport by projecting to one dimension or onto a tree
metric, where the optimal coupling is available in closed form. It is worth stating explicitly why
these are not competitors here: their speed comes precisely from the absence of a filtration to
respect. The contribution of the present work is to keep a closed-form coupling inside a nested
recursion, which is where the difficulty lies.

4.2
Construction
Three choices in Algorithm 1 deserve comment.
Fixed grid rather than data-adaptive cells.
Cell boundaries and representatives are deter-
ministic given (δ, s). Cells built from sample quantiles are more accurate in some regimes (Sec-
tion 6.4), but they make the cell boundaries random, and then the consistency proof of Section 5
no longer covers the estimator that is actually run. We chose the estimator the theorem covers.
Window of length k.
Conditioning on the last k cell indices bounds the number of nodes by
(number of cells)k, independent of T. Section 6.3 shows k is a two-sided knob with an interior
optimum.
Fallback on unvisited cells.
If a source cell is never visited the count ratio is 0/0. The estimator
returns the uniform row there.
This is not cosmetic: it is what makes every estimated row a
probability vector at every sample size, which the consistency proof requires (Remark 5).
4.3
Cost
The number of visited segments grows like n1.24 at T = 10 and n1.34 at T = 50 (Table 2), i.e.
sub-quadratic but super-linear, and the exponent itself drifts upward with depth. A configuration
with n = 105, T = 100 completes in about ten minutes of laptop CPU. This is a cost milestone
and not an accuracy milestone; Section 6.2 explains why the two must be reported separately.

Theory
5.1
Consistency
Let ˆKM be the count-based kernel estimator from M observed transition pairs with the uniform
fallback row, and K0 the true kernel.
Assumption 2 (Reachability). Every source cell has strictly positive probability under the law of
the first coordinate of a transition pair.
Assumption 3 (Sampling). The pooled transition pairs are pairwise independent, identically
distributed and measurable.
Theorem 4 (Consistency). Under Assumptions 2 and 3 and a bounded cost, the backward-recursion
value computed from ˆKM converges almost surely, as M →∞, to the value computed from K0.
Proof sketch. The argument is a continuous-mapping argument with an explicit modulus. (i) The
c.d.f. is 1-Lipschitz in the mass vector, hence by (1) each comonotone weight is Lipschitz in (p, q)
with constant 2; (ii) since the coupling cost is linear in the cost matrix and the weights are bounded,
|QC(p, q; M) −QC(p′, q′; M)| ≤2nmB(∥p −p′∥1 + ∥q −q′∥1); (iii) one recursion step is jointly
Lipschitz in the kernel and in the continuation value; (iv) composing s steps gives a Lipschitz
bound with an explicit, recursively defined constant; (v) a Lipschitz bound whose input vanishes
yields convergence of the value; (vi) the strong law of large numbers gives almost-sure convergence
of empirical frequencies, and under Assumption 2 the ratio of frequencies converges to the true
kernel.
5.2
Machine-checked formalisation
The proof above is formalised in Lean 4 with mathlib. The development is 35 results, 613 lines,
zero sorry, and #print axioms reports only propext, Classical.choice and Quot.sound for
every statement, including Theorem 4.
Remark 5 (What formalisation forced into the open). Three hypotheses that an informal proof
leaves implicit had to be stated. (i) Reachability. Without Assumption 2 the estimated kernel is
undefined on unvisited cells and the ratio has no limit. (ii) The fallback row. For finite M the
denominator can vanish, so the estimator must return a genuine probability vector in that case;
otherwise the hypothesis of the composition step fails at small M and the induction does not start.

A non-Gaussian limit at ties
Proposition 7. Let p = (0.2, 0.5, 0.3) and q = (0.2, 0.6, 0.2) on {0, 1, 2} with cost |x −y|, so that
the two c.d.f.s coincide at the first cut point. Let ˆV be the comonotone cost between independent
empirical versions of p and q. Then

whose mean is 0.4514 and is in particular nonzero.
The mechanism is that (1) is directionally but not fully differentiable: where two c.d.f.s coin-
cide, the min/max structure makes the first-order expansion a nonlinear function of the Gaussian
fluctuation. Simulation with R = 200,000 replicates at N = 50,000 gives mean 0.4504 (hand deriva-

p < 10−12.
The practical consequence is sharper than “the limit is non-normal”: a confidence interval built
on a naive normal approximation is mis-centred, not merely mis-scaled, and no choice of variance
estimator repairs it.

Experiments
Protocol.
All timings are on the same machine. Where a competitor is compared we run their
published code on their instance generator; a re-implementation is used only to understand mecha-
nism, never to claim a ratio. Ground truth is analytic where available and otherwise exact dynamic
programming with a linear-programming inner solver. Every number in the tables is generated
from the result files at build time.
6.1
Given a scenario tree
ATSW is three to four orders of magnitude cheaper. The honest counterpart is the error column:
replacing the linear program by a comonotone coupling costs roughly ten to twenty per cent on
these instances, and we have no bound on that gap.
The entropic branch of the competitor’s own code is not a usable middle ground here. Its docu-
mentation states that the method should converge to unregularised transport as ε →0, while warn-
ing that values below roughly ε = 0.01 may cause problems in practice; our sweep (Appendix C)
is consistent with that warning and shows the practical range to be narrow — the relative error is

6.2
Paths only: an informational limit
Full-history conditioning is not merely inaccurate, it is inaccurate in a way that more data does
not fix: fitting the three measured points gives a bias decaying like n−0.19, so five per cent would
require on the order of 4 × 109 paths. The cause is that the number of realisable histories grows
like (number of cells)T , so at depth almost every history is observed once and the conditional laws
are estimated from a single sample.
Two natural repairs fail, and their failure is informative. Coarsening the grid with depth is
much worse (+460% at the most aggressive setting) because coarse cell centres introduce a cost
error that dominates the sampling gain. Data-adaptive quantile cells do not help either, because
they change where the cells are but not how many histories there are.

6.4
Fixed grid versus data-adaptive cells
This is a deliberate trade, not an improvement: we accept more bias in the under-conditioned
regime in exchange for an estimator the theorem covers. The grid width has an interior optimum
for the same sampling reason as the window length — on the Gaussian benchmark δ = 0.0625 is
worse than δ = 0.125.
6.5
Selecting k and δ
Not yet written.
Both parameters have interior optima and both are currently chosen by sweep against a ground
truth that a user will not have. The target is a selection rule computable from the sample alone —
a held-out split on paths, or a stability criterion across grid shifts — validated against the swept
optimum on the benchmarks of this section. What would falsify it: a rule whose selected (k, δ) is
worse than a fixed default across the benchmark set.

Demonstration application
Not yet written.
The experiments above are benchmarks against ground truth, which establishes correctness but
not usefulness. One application will be developed in full. Two candidates, both verified to be open
in the literature survey:
(A) Evaluation metric for generative time-series models. Current practice compares marginal or
two-point statistics, which are blind to information flow; a generator that leaks future information
scores well. Protocol: train several generators on the same data, rank them by ATSW against held-
out paths, and check the ranking against a downstream sequential decision task. What would falsify
the claim: ATSW ranking no better than a static Wasserstein baseline at predicting downstream
performance.
(B) Reward from expert trajectories in imitation learning. Existing OT-based rewards use static
transport between state-visitation distributions. Protocol: substitute ATSW and measure sample
efficiency against the static baseline on standard control tasks. What would falsify the claim: no
improvement once the static baseline is given the same amount of tuning.
Option (A) is closer to the estimator’s guarantees; option (B) has the wider audience.
8
Discussion
8.1
What the negative result means
It is tempting to read Section 6.2 as a defect of ATSW. It is not: any estimator that sees only paths
faces the same count of realisable histories, and the model-based competitor beats it precisely by
using information ATSW is not given. The useful reading is that adapted transport from paths
alone is feasible only under a conditioning restriction, and that choosing the restriction — rather
than choosing the coupling — is where the accuracy is decided.
8.2
Limitations
 The comonotone coupling is not optimal on multi-branch trees; on the scenario-tree bench-
mark this costs ten to twenty per cent, and we have no bound.
 Neither k nor δ has a data-driven selection rule; both have interior optima.

9
Conclusion
Adapted transport from sampled paths is feasible, but the binding constraint is statistical rather
than computational, and the remedy is to condition on a bounded window rather than to improve
the coupling. The resulting estimator is consistent, and that statement is machine-checked rather
than argued.
